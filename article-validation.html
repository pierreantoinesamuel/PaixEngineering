VALIDATION / JUN 24, 2026 / 06 MIN READ

Test plans should
answer a question.

A test is not valuable because it produces a number. It is valuable because that number helps a team make a decision.

Before writing a validation plan, name the uncertainty. Is the enclosure strong enough? Will the mechanism survive repeated use? Can a supplier hold the tolerance at volume? Each question points toward a different test, a different fixture, and a different definition of success.

This simple step keeps validation from becoming a collection of disconnected activities. It also helps the team choose the right level of fidelity. Early prototypes may only need a directional measurement to compare two concepts. Later builds need repeatable setups, controlled variables, and enough samples to reveal variation.

The best plans connect three things: the risk being explored, the evidence that will reduce it, and the decision that follows. When a result falls outside the expected range, the team should already know what changes next. That might mean revisiting a material, adjusting a feature, changing the process, or stopping an approach before more money is committed.

Good validation creates momentum because it turns uncertainty into a sequence of manageable questions. It makes learning visible to designers, engineers, manufacturers, and stakeholders alike. Start with the decision you need to make, then build the smallest credible test that can inform it. The result will be more useful than a larger plan built only to look complete.
